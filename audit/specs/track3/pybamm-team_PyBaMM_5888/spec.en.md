## pybamm-team/PyBaMM#5888 fix: convert Ai2020 electrolyte diffusivity from cm2/s to m2/s

- What changed (1–3 sentences, naming files and formulas):
  `electrolyte_diffusivity_Ai2020(c_e,T)` in `packages/pybamm/src/pybamm/input/parameters/lithium_ion/Ai2020.py` multiplies the original `10**(-4.43-54/(T-229-0.005*c_e)-0.00022*c_e)` by `1e-4`, converting cm²/s to m²/s; the documented units of the diffusivity and the conductivity are also corrected, and the conductivity formula is unchanged. `packages/pybamm/tests/unit/test_parameters/test_parameter_sets/test_Ai2020.py` adds numerical values for the diffusivity and the conductivity and a comparison with Xu2019, and tightens the relative tolerance of the existing parameter tests; `CHANGELOG.md` records the fix.

- Invariants (mathematical statements; write them as checkable equalities/inequalities, give variables, sign conventions, domain of validity):
  Here \(c\) is the numerical value of the electrolyte salt concentration in mol/m³, and \(T\) is the numerical value of the absolute temperature in K; \(D_A\) and \(D_X\) are the diffusion coefficients returned by the public parameter interfaces of Ai2020 and Xu2019 respectively, in m²/s. Let \(C=c/1000\) be the numerical value of the concentration in mol/L, and \(q=T-229-0.005c\). Below take \(c\geq0,q>0\); the numerical check is further restricted to the rectangular region below; these are invariants of an empirical constitutive formula, not a universal law for all electrolytes.

  1. **Primary — SI units and the absolute scale of the constitutive formula.**
     \[
     D_A(c,T)=10^{-4}\exp\!\left[\ln(10)\left(-4.43-\frac{54}{T-229-5C}-0.22C\right)\right]\quad\mathrm{m^2/s}.
     \]
     Equivalently, \(\log_{10}[D_A/(1\,\mathrm{m^2/s})]=-8.43-54/q-0.00022c\). The physical unit identity is \(1\,\mathrm{cm^2/s}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\), and the concentration unit conversion is \(1000\,\mathrm{mol/m^3}=1\,\mathrm{mol/L}\). In particular, \(D_A(1000,298.15)\approx3.2227\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\); this approximate value is a rounded check point, and the full equality is the benchmark.

  2. **Secondary — positive diffusivity and the temperature and concentration response of this fit.** For the above region, \(0<D_A<\infty\), and
     \[
     \partial_T\ln[D_A/(1\,\mathrm{m^2/s})]=\ln(10)\frac{54}{q^2}>0,
     \qquad
     \partial_c\ln[D_A/(1\,\mathrm{m^2/s})]=-\ln(10)\left(\frac{0.27}{q^2}+0.00022\right)<0.
     \]
     Hence at fixed concentration the diffusivity strictly increases on heating, and at fixed temperature it strictly decreases on increasing the salt concentration. The positive sign is consistent with the Fick diffusive flux \(\mathbf N_{\rm diff}=-D_A\nabla c\), with \(\mathbf N_{\rm diff}\cdot\nabla c=-D_A\lVert\nabla c\rVert^2\leq0\); this refers only to the diffusive flux component, and no such assertion is made for the total flux including migration and convection. Checking these signs alone cannot identify the constant-factor error corrected by this PR; the primary must be checked at the same time.

  3. **Secondary — the same transport fit is consistent between parameter sets.** For the same SI inputs, \(D_A(c,T)=D_X(c,T)\). Only the same Valøen–Reimers diffusivity fit used by both is compared; the two complete battery parameter sets or discharge trajectories are not required to be the same.

- Sources of the invariants (first principles / literature formula / consistency with other implementations in the library / dimensions):
  The unit factor of the primary comes from the square of the length unit; the source of the empirical formula is Valøen and Reimers (2005), *Transport properties of LiPF6-based Li-ion battery electrolytes*, JES 152, A882–A891, Eq. 14, DOI `10.1149/1.1872737`, cited by the merged source. The full text of the paper was not read this time, and the original experimental calibration range and the paper's notation were not independently verified; the attribution of the formula is based on the [Ai2020 merged source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pybamm-team/PyBaMM/343ece2062dbb39789dbfa977e0f2daf96e9e370/packages/pybamm/src/pybamm/input/parameters/lithium_ion/Ai2020.py). The temperature and concentration derivatives are direct mathematical corollaries of the formula above; positive diffusivity is consistent with the down-gradient direction of Fick's law. The [Xu2019 merged source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pybamm-team/PyBaMM/343ece2062dbb39789dbfa977e0f2daf96e9e370/packages/pybamm/src/pybamm/input/parameters/lithium_ion/Xu2019.py) first divides the concentration by 1000, then computes the same fit and multiplies by `1e-4`, and also explicitly states that base-10 exponents are used. It provides in-library consistency evidence, but that two implementations are identical cannot independently prove that the empirical fit agrees with real electrolytes.

- Common operating domain (range of each input, with the basis):
  Suggested common spot-check region: \(c\in[500,1500]\,\mathrm{mol/m^3}\) (0.5–1.5 mol/L), \(T\in[278.15,318.15]\,\mathrm K\) (5–45 °C). The basis is the concentration and temperature end points covered by the three test points of this PR, and the default initial concentration of 1000 mol/m³ and initial temperature of 298.15 K of Ai2020; other combinations inside the rectangle are spot checks supplemented by this audit, not combinations already tested by the PR, nor a literature applicability interval that has been verified. In this interval \(q\in[41.65,86.65]\,\mathrm K\), far from the singularity of the denominator. Negative concentrations, direct input in degrees Celsius, \(q\leq0\), and extrapolation approaching the singularity are excluded; the interface is not required to throw an exception for these illegal physical inputs. By monotonicity one can also check \(D_A(1500,278.15)\leq D_A(c,T)\leq D_A(500,318.15)\), with the two end points computed by the independent formula of the primary.

- Numerical check method (which public API of the merged commit, what to compute, what to compare against, suggested tolerance and why; if finite differences are needed, the step-size strategy):
  The following is a scheme for later execution; no code was run this time. Use `p = pybamm.ParameterValues("Ai2020")` of the merged version, and obtain the scalar value through `p.evaluate(p["Electrolyte diffusivity [m2.s-1]"](pybamm.Scalar(c), pybamm.Scalar(T)))`; evaluate `ParameterValues("Xu2019")` in the same way. This calling pattern is directly confirmed by the merged tests, and there is no need to build a DFN or run the discharge solver.

  Evaluate at the four corners of the rectangle, the default point, the PR's three points, and at least 100 uniformly random points with a fixed seed. For the primary, compute the benchmark with the independent `exp(log(10)*...)` form and the mol/L input conversion, without calling the function under test as the oracle; first assert finite and strictly positive, then compare the relative error \(\lvert D_A/D_{\rm ref}-1\rvert\leq10^{-12}\). Compare Xu2019 with the same relative tolerance and an explicit `atol=0`; a small, smooth double-precision expression has no ill-conditioned denominator in this interval, and `1e-12` suffices to accommodate rounding from operation order while strictly checking the SI scale. The rounded check point `3.2227e-10` uses only `rtol=1e-3, atol=0`, and must not be assigned the precision of the full formula.

  For the second item, let \(L(c,T)=\ln[D_A(c,T)/(1\,\mathrm{m^2/s})]\), and compute with central differences \([L(c,T+h_T)-L(c,T-h_T)]/(2h_T)\) and the corresponding concentration expression. Start with \(h_T=0.01\,\mathrm K\) and \(h_c=0.1\,\mathrm{mol/m^3}\), then halve; the results at both step sizes are compared with the analytic derivative, with a suggested `rtol=1e-5, atol=0`, and the signs are verified. The smallest denominator in this interval is 41.65 K, and the leading truncation relative error of the temperature central difference is about \((h_T/q)^2<6\times10^{-8}\), so this tolerance has ample margin. Take central differences only when the perturbed points remain inside the rectangle; at the boundary use inward points or compare only function values, and if the error is unstable as the step shrinks, record it and check for cancellation error, but do not relax the scale test. The sign assertions can also directly check monotonicity with pairs of points at least 1 K or 10 mol/m³ apart.

- Do the PR's own tests already check this invariant (yes/partial/no, naming test files and assertions):
  **Primary: partial.** `TestAi2020.test_functions` in the [merged `test_Ai2020.py`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pybamm-team/PyBaMM/343ece2062dbb39789dbfa977e0f2daf96e9e370/packages/pybamm/tests/unit/test_parameters/test_parameter_sets/test_Ai2020.py) asserts `pytest.approx(3.2227e-10, rel=1e-3, abs=0)` for the diffusivity at `(1000,298.15)`, which does constrain the physical magnitude and can detect the old `1e4`-fold error, but does not cover the shape of the function within the region. It also adds a single-point assertion of the conductivity `1.1943`; the conductivity implementation has no behavior change, so it is not additionally listed as an invariant of this audit.

  **Positivity, response derivatives and region bounds: no.** The newly added tests do not explicitly check these properties. **Consistency across parameter sets: partial.** `TestAi2020.test_electrolyte_diffusivity` calls `np.testing.assert_allclose(..., rtol=1e-12)` at `(1000,298.15)`, `(500,318.15)`, `(1500,278.15)`, but does not set `atol`. NumPy's default `atol=1e-8` is larger than the diffusivity under test, so this assertion cannot be interpreted as a genuine `1e-12` relative consistency test; for example a zero value could also pass this comparison. The old value of about `1e-6` would still fail, and the independent single-point test above does prevent the default point from degenerating to zero. Only the PR tests read are evaluated here, and no claim is made to have reviewed the whole test suite.

- Do you expect the change to satisfy it (satisfies / may not satisfy / uncertain), with one or two sentences of reasoning (speculation, not a conclusion):

- Formalization (Lean) feasibility: can the invariant be written as a proposition over finite-dimensional reals, and what are the main obstacles:
  **Yes.** Taking \((c,T)\in\mathbb R^2\), exact decimal constants as rational numbers, and writing \(10^x\) as `Real.exp (Real.log 10 * x)`, and assuming \(q>0\), one can formalize the SI factor, the equality of the two concentration expressions, positivity, the signs of the partial derivatives, and the end-point bounds on the rectangle. The main proof work is nonzero denominators, exponential/logarithm and differentiation rules, and strict inequalities; the numerical rounded check point needs interval bounds rather than an equality. Physical units need explicit dimensional wrappers or unit-conversion definitions; the experimental correctness of the empirical fit cannot be proved by real-number algebra, and a real-number formula proof does not automatically cover the PyBaMM symbolic evaluation path or IEEE-754 rounding, which require a separate implementation correspondence and error proof.
